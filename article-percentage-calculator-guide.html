How to Calculate Percentages: Formula, Increase, Decrease and Examples

By EasyWebTools Editorial Team · Published November 25, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026

Learn the percentage formula, how to calculate discounts and percentage change, and how to avoid common mistakes.

What a percentage represents

A percentage is a way to express a part of a whole as a number out of 100. The word percent literally means per hundred. If 25 out of 100 items have a particular feature, that is 25 percent. Percentages are useful because they let people compare proportions even when the original totals are different. They appear in discounts, taxes, test scores, survey results, business reports, interest rates and everyday comparisons. Before calculating, identify which value is the whole and which value is the part.

The basic percentage formula

To find what percentage one number is of another, divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100. For example, if 18 out of 60 questions are correct, calculate 18 divided by 60 and multiply by 100 to get 30 percent. To find a percentage of a number, convert the percentage to a decimal by dividing by 100, then multiply by the number. For instance, 15 percent of 200 is 0.15 multiplied by 200, which equals 30.

How to find a percentage of a value

This is one of the most common percentage questions. First write down the percentage you want, then divide it by 100. Multiply the resulting decimal by the original value. A 20 percent discount on a $50 item is 0.20 multiplied by 50, or $10. That amount is the discount, not the final price. Subtract it from the original price to get $40 before any additional taxes or fees.

Percentage increase and decrease

Percentage change compares a new value with an original value. Subtract the original value from the new value, divide that difference by the original value, and multiply by 100. A positive result indicates an increase and a negative result indicates a decrease. If a monthly bill rises from $80 to $92, the difference is $12; dividing $12 by $80 gives 0.15, or a 15 percent increase. The original value is the baseline.

Why the original value matters

The same numerical difference can represent very different percentage changes depending on the starting point. An increase of 10 from 20 is 50 percent, while an increase of 10 from 100 is 10 percent. That is why percentage change uses the original value as the denominator. If the original value is zero, the calculation is undefined because division by zero is not possible. In that situation, describe the absolute change rather than inventing a percentage.

Percentage points are different

A percentage-point change is the arithmetic difference between two percentages. If a rate moves from 20 percent to 25 percent, it rises by 5 percentage points. The relative percentage increase is 25 percent because the change of 5 is divided by the original 20. These two descriptions answer different questions. Reports about interest rates, polling, conversion rates and other percentages should use the correct term to avoid exaggerating or understating a change.

Practical examples

For a shopping discount, calculate the percentage amount first and subtract it from the listed price. For a test score, divide correct answers by total questions and multiply by 100. For a monthly metric, compare the new month with the previous month using the previous month as the original value. For a recipe, calculate a percentage of an ingredient quantity and keep the measurement units consistent. Each example uses the same arithmetic, but the meaning of the inputs changes.

Rounding and accuracy

A calculator may display several decimal places, while a receipt, report or spreadsheet may require a particular rounding rule. Do not round too early if you are doing multiple calculations, because small rounding differences can accumulate. For money, follow the currency and accounting rules that apply. For scientific or statistical work, keep enough significant digits and document the method. The percentage formula is straightforward, but the context determines how the result should be reported.

Common mistakes to avoid

A frequent error is dividing by the new value instead of the original value when calculating percentage change. Another is confusing the discount amount with the final price. People also sometimes describe a percentage-point difference as a percentage increase. Write down the question in words before entering numbers: what is the part, what is the whole, and what is the baseline? That short check prevents many errors.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate 20 percent of 50?

Convert 20 percent to 0.20 and multiply by 50. The result is 10.

How do I calculate percentage increase?

Subtract the original value from the new value, divide by the original value and multiply by 100.

What is a percentage point?

It is the arithmetic difference between two percentages, not the relative percentage change.

Can percentage change start from zero?

No. The standard formula divides by the original value, so a zero baseline makes the percentage change undefined.
